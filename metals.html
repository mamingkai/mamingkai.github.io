Rust as a sponge: how iron minerals grab poisonous metals

Invisible poisons

Lead in drinking water, cadmium in rice paddies, copper in mining runoff: heavy metals are invisible, persistent, and harmful at low concentrations. Cadmium contamination in Japan in the last century caused itai-itai disease, named for the cries of its victims. Whether such metals end up in a river, in a crop or safely stuck in the ground depends largely on what the soil does with them.

The humble hero: iron oxide

Soils and sediments are full of iron oxides, essentially microscopic rust. Their surfaces are covered with reactive sites, each a tiny hook of oxygen and hydrogen, ready to bind a metal ion passing by. Where these hooks hold on, the metal is immobilised. Where conditions change, they let go. Rust, it turns out, is one of nature's best water purifiers.

Turning chemistry into a calculator

A single bottle experiment tells you how much metal sticks under one set of conditions. A surface complexation model tells you why, and predicts what happens everywhere else. It treats the mineral surface as a set of chemical sites, accounts for the electric charge that builds up on it as pH changes, and writes down the reactions between metals and sites as equilibrium equations, just like reactions in solution. Fit the model to a few experiments and it can forecast adsorption across pH, concentration and salinity.

Why pH rules everything

Raise the pH and the oxide surface turns more negative, pulling in positively charged metal ions like a magnet; lower it and the same metals are pushed off. Between the two lies a sharp transition, the adsorption edge, where a small change in pH switches a metal from stuck to free. That is why acidifying a soil, or acid mine drainage, can flush metals out.

From models to clean-up

Models like these help judge remediation strategies, for example adding iron minerals to contaminated soil, and warn when a once-safe sink could turn into a source. They also taught me a way of thinking that I use in all my later work: describe the surface honestly, write down the chemistry, and let the numbers show what the system is trying to tell you.
