A bacterium, a mineral, and the force that holds them together

Soil is crowded

A single teaspoon of healthy soil holds more bacteria than there are people on Earth, all of them bumping into mineral grains. Most of those minerals are coated in iron oxides, the stuff of rust and ochre. Does a bacterium that lands on such a surface simply drift away, or does it grab on? The answer decides surprisingly large things: whether minerals dissolve, whether pollutants stay bound, whether carbon is stored for centuries or eaten in weeks.

A mineral named after a poet

The iron oxide I worked with is goethite, named in 1806 after the German poet Johann Wolfgang von Goethe, who was also a keen mineral collector. It is among the most common iron minerals on the planet, found in soils, sediments and even the brown crusts of old iron-rich rocks. Its needle-shaped crystals expose a huge surface to the world, which makes it a magnet for nutrients, metals, organic matter and microbes.

Measuring a force you cannot see

To measure how hard a single bacterium sticks, I used atomic force microscopy. An AFM feels a surface the way a record player feels a groove: a flexible cantilever with a tip only nanometres wide is brought towards the sample, and a laser bounced off the cantilever reveals the tiniest bend. Put a bacterial cell on that tip, press it onto goethite and pull away, and the bend records the pull-off force, a few billionths of a newton. Repeat it hundreds of times and a picture of the interaction appears.

The bacterium

I used Pseudomonas putida, a common, versatile soil bacterium that is a workhorse of environmental microbiology. Its surface is a complex landscape of proteins, sugars and charged groups, and each of them can bond differently to the mineral. Which one wins depends on chemistry such as pH and salt, which is why this is as much a chemistry question as a biology one.

From one cell to a whole ecosystem

It might seem a long way from one cell on one tip to an entire soil. But adhesion is the first step in biofilms, in microbial reduction of iron, and in the formation of the organic-mineral associations that lock carbon away. Understanding the smallest handshake between a microbe and a mineral gives us a foothold for understanding all of those.
