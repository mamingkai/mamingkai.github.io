Phosphate, iron and a race against oxygen

The element that feeds us, and chokes our lakes

Phosphorus was discovered in 1669 by a German alchemist who boiled down huge quantities of urine, hoping to find gold. What he found glowed in the dark. Three and a half centuries later, phosphorus is the quiet backbone of life, part of every strand of DNA and every molecule of cellular energy, and the reason modern farms can feed billions. It is also a problem with two faces: phosphate rock is mined from a limited number of places, and the phosphate that washes off fields into lakes feeds algae blooms that suffocate fish and ruin drinking water.

Iron, the quiet bodyguard

Nature has a built-in defence. In most lakes, iron minerals in the sediment grab phosphate and hold it in the mud, like a bodyguard keeping a troublemaker out of the room. Water managers have copied this: add iron-rich material to a lake, and the sediment keeps more phosphate. The catch is that the bodyguard sleeps on the job when oxygen runs out.

When the oxygen goes, the story turns

In deep, oxygen-free sediment, iron oxides are reduced and bacteria produce sulfide, the rotten-egg smell of swamps. Sulfide attacks iron minerals, and the phosphate they carried can be set free. Whether it stays free, drifts into the lake, or is caught again depends on a race between reactions. My PhD, as part of the EU Marie Curie network P-TRAP, set out to measure that race: how fast phosphate is bound, how fast it is released, and what decides the outcome.

A beautiful blue second chance

One of the endings is a mineral called vivianite, an iron phosphate that is colourless when freshly formed and turns a deep blue-green on contact with air. If phosphate is released but meets enough iron, vivianite can form and trap it again, even without oxygen. Understanding when this happens is exciting for two reasons: it can protect lakes, and vivianite has been proposed as a way to recover phosphate from sediments and wastewater.

Life in the oxygen-free lab

Most of my work happened in sealed glove boxes and gas-tight reactors, where a puff of air could ruin weeks of experiments. I learned to build my own tools, from auto-samplers to gas-tight bags, and travelled to Bayreuth to learn Mössbauer spectroscopy, a technique that tells us which form of iron we really have. I also worked alongside fellow PhD researchers in the network, sharing protocols, field work and synchrotron beamtime.

Why it matters

Lakes will keep receiving phosphate for as long as we farm, and restoration measures need to work for decades, not seasons.
